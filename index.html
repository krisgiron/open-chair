Pull up a chair

A layoff isn't a verdict on your worth.

It's a business decision.

But when the paychecks stop and the rent, groceries, and gas don't, it becomes personal.

I've been laid off. I know how lonely and uncertain it gets, even with people who love you in your corner. So I started The Open Chair for anyone in the same spot who doesn't know where to turn or what to do next. A coffee, a conversation, and a focus on what's next, not what happened. Because a little forward progress is how people get back on their feet.

If your search is taking longer than expected, you're not imagining it.

27.1%

More than 1 in 4 unemployed people had been searching for over six months in September 2026,3 up from 23.6% a year earlier.4

Employers announced more than 1.2 million job cuts in 2025,1 the most since 2020, and another 573,195 through September of this year.2

You're not alone in this.

What is the Open Chair?

Free career help for anyone going through a layoff. Sometimes that's one conversation and a resume pass. Sometimes it's a full rewrite, LinkedIn updates, and interview prep. In person in the Bay Area or over video.

I'm not a recruiter, so I don't place people in jobs. I help with everything around the search.

No course, nothing to sign up for, and nothing sold to you at the end of it.

Why is it free?

I don't think it's right to charge someone who's just been laid off.

If your layoff felt impersonal, I want the search for what's next to feel personal. That's what the Open Chair is for.

How it started

When I was a kid, I wanted to be a teacher. My fourth grade teacher, Ms. Miller, was also my volleyball and basketball coach, and the way she taught and believed in me inspired me to want to do the same for others when I got older.

Life had other plans. I didn't become a teacher, but I coached basketball, and then spent 25 years building customer support and operations teams for HR and payroll companies. The part I'm proudest of was never the work itself. It was helping the people on my teams grow their careers.

In May, the startup where I led customer operations restructured, and my role was cut. So I made a sign, carried it into a coffee shop, and set it on my table: "Got laid off? You're not alone. Let's chat!" Then people started sitting down.

What started as an experiment became my mission: to be the coach I wish I'd had during my own layoffs.

How we'll work together

  1. 1
    I'll go first. I believe in being vulnerable, so I'll tell you about my own layoffs before I ask about yours.
  2. 2
    Start with you. Before anything else, I want to know how you're doing and hear your story. The resume can wait.
  3. 3
    Forward, not backward. We'll talk about what happened, but we spend our time on what's next.
  4. 4
    Real feedback. I'll tell you what isn't working and what is.

Started a new job after ten years at one company

I can't promise you a job. But I can promise you won't have to look for one alone.

Most people only see hiring from one seat at the table. I've sat in all of them.

And right now, I'm laid off and looking, just like you. I've been the hiring manager, the interviewer, the one scoping roles with recruiting and HR, and the one delivering the hard news.

I was an early employee at PayCycle, which Intuit acquired, and later at Gusto, and one of seven on the founding team that launched Salsa. Along the way, I trained managers from within my own teams, because growing people was the part I loved most.

Hiring was the other half. Individualization (StrengthsFinder) is one of my top strengths, so I'm always looking for what makes each person unique, what they do best, and how they'd help a team grow. I've built hiring processes from the ground up, from the job description to the offer call with an excited candidate. So when we work on your resume and interviews, we'll look at them the way a hiring manager will.

When the answer is no

Only one person gets the offer. And everyone else? They usually get the same generic email:

Re: Your application "After careful consideration..."

No reason, no feedback, nothing to help them prepare for the next one. I've sent that email in the past, and I've worked hard ever since to be more thoughtful about how I follow up with candidates who didn't get the role. I believe everyone deserves transparency into the hiring process. So when candidates ask for feedback, I give it, and sometimes I offer it when they don't.

Rejections are one of the hardest parts of a search. When one comes in, we'll walk back through the interview and look for what you can do better next time. If the email won't tell you, we'll figure it out ourselves.

That's exactly how the Open Chair works. When you sit down with me, I'll look for the good in you, and I'll be honest about what needs work.
